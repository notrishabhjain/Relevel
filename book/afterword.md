summary: What Anaya knows at the end, what she does not, and where a reader who has finished the story might go next.

In September, a year and a half after the night she began it, Anaya Deshmukh scrolled back to the top of the decision log and read the first entry. *14 March. Decision: work on the personal-details problem first. Ticket themes second. Evidence: 37 of 400 conversations in one week contained an identity number, a tax number, a bank account or a full address. My count; Farah can check it.*

She read the last line twice. *I would change my mind if.* Of the many things the year had taught her, that line had been the cheapest and had done the most work.

Behind her, on the whiteboard, Imran had left a drawing in three colours of everything that now sat between a customer's keyboard and the person who read the reply: a shape for each thing the guard did, an arrow for each place a decision was made, a star beside each place where a wrong answer would cost the most. It was not beautiful. It was exactly what she had built.

She could say now what she had not been able to say in March. She knew what the machine was, in plain words, and what it could not do. She knew how to ask whether it worked, with a number. She knew what it cost, and who would pay. She knew a good many ways it could fail, and had a few of them written down, and she knew what it was not allowed to do alone. She knew that people she had never met had used it, and that some of them had been unimpressed, which was the useful part.

She also knew a good deal that she did not know. The guard had been measured in English, in Hindi and in the mixture of the two, on messages the team had written itself. It had not been measured in Tamil or Bengali or Telugu, or in the writing of customers outside two cities, or against an attacker with more patience than Imran. It had never been called compliant by anyone who understood the word, and it would be a mistake for anyone to call it that on its behalf. It was a careful tool with honest numbers. That was all it had ever claimed to be, and it was enough to be getting on with.

## What the book has tried to do

This book followed one small project through the whole of an education, because a project gives every idea somewhere to land. It has told the story of a single company, in a single city, with invented people and invented figures. The prices are made up. The messages are made up. The measurements are what a careful team might find, and not what any real team has found. Where a number mattered, it was there to show how a number is reasoned about, and you should check it against your own cases before you borrow it.

It has also tried to give you the words. Nearly everything in a field like this goes by a name, and a good part of the difficulty of learning it is that the names arrive faster than the things they stand for. Each chapter began with a few new words and used them, in that chapter and in all the later ones, in the same sense. If a word has slipped away from you, the glossary at the back keeps every one of them, with a short plain definition and a note of the chapter where it first appeared.

## Where to go from here

A book can give you the picture. It cannot give you the habit, and the habit is most of the skill. What you have read here becomes yours when you do it: when you write the plain description of a problem and find that it does not hold together, when you build the small answer key and see how often you were wrong, when you break your own system on purpose and watch what it does.

The course app is built for exactly that. Every chapter of the book has a companion in the app, linked from the top of the chapter page, with the same ideas in the same order, and with the work to do: the tasks and the questions that you answer and check. Read a chapter here and then do it there, or the other way about. The two were written to be walked together.

If you find a place where the story and the course disagree, or where a word is used before it is explained, please say so. The book was checked for exactly that, line by line, and a checker is no substitute for a reader.

And if you are, as Anaya was, the person in the room who has been asked to make sense of something nobody quite understands, take a notebook, and begin it with one line about what you noticed. Everything else follows from that.
