In September, a year and a half after the night she began it, Anaya Deshmukh scrolled to the top of the decision log and read the first entry. It was dated 14 March. The decision was to work on the personal-details problem first and ticket themes second. The evidence was that 37 of 400 conversations in one week contained an identity number, a tax number, a bank account or a full address, by her own count, which Farah could check. She read the last line twice: "I would change my mind if". Of the many things the year had taught her, that line had been the cheapest and had done the most work.

## Where the case ends

By September the guard ran on every chat in two support teams and in the trial of a lending partner. Behind Anaya on the whiteboard Imran had left a drawing in three colours of everything that now stood between a customer's keyboard and the person who read the reply. It showed a shape for each thing the guard did, an arrow for each place where a decision was made, and a star beside each place where a wrong answer would cost the most. It was not elegant. It was an accurate account of what had been built.

## What the seven parts add up to

The book was arranged in seven parts, and each added a kind of ability. They are listed here as a reader might check them.

| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The problem worth solving | 1 to 7 | Turn an impression into a count, test an idea against the four product risks, learn from users by asking about the past, size a market from the bottom up, choose what not to do, plan in outcomes, and write a specification whose "done" can be tested |
| 2. Meeting the machine | 8 to 13 | Say what a language model is and is not, read a request and its cost, explain why a chatbot's memory is the application's, write and test instructions, and measure with an answer key |

## What the project still does not know

A textbook that ends with certainty has usually left something out. The guard had been measured in English, in Hindi and in the mixture of the two, on messages that the team itself had written. It had not been measured in Tamil, Bengali or Telugu, or in the writing of customers outside two cities, or against an attacker with more patience than Imran. Nobody who understood the word had called it compliant, and it would be a mistake for anyone to say so on its behalf. It was a careful tool with honest numbers, which was all it had claimed to be.

The same caution applies to the book. Sahaj, its people, its prices and its figures are invented. The tools and techniques are real, and the failures are of the kind that occur in real systems. Where a number matters, it is there to show how a number is reasoned about, and a reader should check it against their own cases before borrowing it.

## Words

Nearly everything in this field goes by a name, and much of the difficulty of learning it is that the names arrive faster than the things they stand for. Each chapter introduced a few terms and used them, in that chapter and in all later ones, in the same sense. The glossary at the back lists every term with a short definition and the chapter where it first appeared.

## Using the book with the course app

A book can provide the picture, and it cannot provide the habit, which is most of the skill. The material here becomes a reader's own through practice: writing the plain description of a problem and finding that it does not hold together, building a small answer key and seeing how often one was wrong, breaking a system on purpose and watching what it does.

The course app is built for that practice. Every chapter of the book has a companion in the app, linked beneath the chapter, with the same ideas in the same order and with tasks and questions to answer and check. A reader can read a chapter here and then do the work there, or work in the other order. The two were written to be used together.

Where the book and the course disagree, or where a term is used before it has been explained, the fault should be reported. The book was checked for exactly that, line by line, but a checker is no substitute for a reader. And for anyone who is, as Anaya was, the person in the room who has been asked to make sense of something that nobody quite understands, the advice is to begin a notebook with one line about what was noticed. Everything else follows from that.
