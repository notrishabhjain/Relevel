On Sunday mornings, Anaya phoned Dr. Meenakshi Rao. Meenakshi had taught her linguistics, the study of language. She was now retired in Bengaluru. In the last week of March, Anaya read out her list of work.

Rewrite the chatbot's greeting. Build a tool to find identity numbers. Extend it to names and addresses. Make it work in Hindi. Make it work in Hinglish. Read photographs. Read voice notes. Sell it to other companies. Build a small version that runs in a browser.

Meenakshi asked which of the nine she would say no to. Anaya said she would do all of them in the end. "Then you have no strategy," said Meenakshi. "You have a hope with a calendar."

This chapter looks at what a strategy is made of, and how a team picks where to begin. It also shows how to rank work that competes for time. The trick is to let a formula help, and not let it decide for you.

## The case: nine good ideas

Think of a wedding guest list with every cousin on it. Nobody is left out, so the list cannot tell you who sits at the front. Every item on Anaya's list was reasonable, and that was the trouble. If nothing on a list is ever refused, the list cannot guide a choice on Monday morning. Any task can be defended.

Meenakshi was not being harsh. She was being practical. A strategy begins when someone is left out.

## Vision, strategy and plan

Meenakshi asked Anaya to say how three things differ. People often mix them up. Think of a journey by train. The vision is the city you want to reach. The strategy is the route you pick, and why. The plan is this week's timetable. The table shows each one for the guard.

Table: Vision, strategy and plan
| | Question it answers | Horizon | The guard's version |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vision | Where are we going? | Three to five years | Every team at every company that talks to customers in India can see at a glance that nothing personal travels further than it should |
| Strategy | How will we win? | One to two years | Start with Sahaj's own chat and nowhere else, and be the best in the country at the mixture of languages people actually type |
| Plan | What will we do now? | This quarter | Ship the number-finding part, in the chat, to the support team, by the end of June |

A vision may be grand. A plan is easy to write. The strategy is the hard part. It must be clear enough to be wrong. And it must leave things out.

## The parts of a strategy

A strategy has three parts. Each is there for a reason.

The first is the *diagnosis*. It is like a doctor's finding before any medicine. It is an honest account of the real problem. It is written so that someone could disagree with it. "We have a privacy problem" is not a diagnosis.

Anaya's version said this. Personal details reach places they should not. Customers type them freely. The chatbot asks for them. Nobody has a way to remove them. And today's tools are built for one writing system, so they fail on the way people here write. It took four drafts before it stopped sounding like a sales pitch.

The second is the *guiding policy*. Think of the house rules. It is the overall approach to the problem, and it includes what you will refuse to do. Hers said this. Catch details by their shape first, because that is cheap and certain. Handle names and addresses next, because that is where other tools fail. Do all of it inside the company, in the way people really write. And do not try to solve everything that could be called privacy.

The third part is a set of coherent actions. That means a few steps that carry out the policy and help each other. She wrote four. She saw that two did not depend on the others, and she cut them.

::: watch A goal is not a strategy
"Become the leading privacy platform in India" is a goal. It has no diagnosis in it. It tells nobody what to do on Monday. Here is a quick test. Take the company's name out of the strategy. Then ask if a rival could paste it into their own paper, and nobody would notice.

A sentence like "use best-in-class technology to protect customer privacy at scale" passes that test. So it is not yet a strategy. A version of it had crept into Anaya's second paragraph. She deleted it.
:::

## Whom it serves, and where to begin

The *value proposition* is the board outside the shop. It says plainly whom the product serves, what job it does for them, and why it is better than what they have. Anaya's positioning statement from the last chapter was the first draft. Now it had to carry weight. The whole strategy rested on it.

Where to begin is a separate choice. Meenakshi's rule was to start small enough to be the best at something. A team can widen later. But it is hard to recover from being only average at everything.

The small first target that a product can clearly win is its *wedge*. Think of the thin end of a wedge going into a log. It should be small enough to be excellent at. And it should join onto something bigger, so that winning it opens a door.

For the guard, the wedge was not "privacy for Indian companies". It was to find and hide the personal details in Sahaj's own support chat, in the three ways people write. That was far smaller than the vision. And it could be won. There was a willing customer inside the company and a real store of messages. There was also Farah Sheikh, who would know within a week whether it worked.

### One number

The wedge was paired with a *North Star*. Sailors steer by one star. A team steers by one sentence and one number. The sentence says what value the customer gets. The number measures it.

Here the sentence was this: messages leave the chat with nothing personal left in them. The number was the share of messages that do. If the number rose, the work was succeeding. If Anaya ever caught herself arguing about some other number, she would stop and ask why.

## Naming the product

Farah heard about the project and stopped at Anaya's desk. Everyone, she said, was calling it "the privacy thing". If it did not get a proper name, it would take whatever name was said first in a meeting. Think of a baby who gets called by the first nickname anyone uses.

The name had to say what the product was and who it was for. It also had to be easy to say to a customer on the phone.

They considered a dozen. Farah thinks in the language of her customers. She proposed Bharat Privacy Guard. She guessed that everyone would shorten it to "the guard" within a week. They chose it, partly because it was exactly as grand as the name on a signboard.

## Ranking the work

The strategy was now in place. Anaya needed an order for the nine items. She used a common scoring method called *RICE*. It is like marks in an exam. Each option gets a score, built from four questions.

- Reach: how many people or messages does it touch in a set period?
- Impact: how much does it change things for each of them?
- Confidence: as a percentage, how sure are you of the first two answers?
- Effort: how many person-weeks will the work take? One person-week is one person working for one week.

Multiply reach, impact and confidence. Then divide by effort. The table shows four of her options.

Table: RICE scores for four of the options
| Option | Reach | Impact | Confidence | Effort (person-weeks) | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rewrite the greeting | 400 | 1 | 80% | 0.1 | 3,200 |
| Find fixed-shape numbers | 400 | 2 | 80% | 2 | 320 |
| Catch photographs of cards | 30 | 3 | 50% | 1 | 45 |
| Find names and addresses in Hinglish | 150 | 2 | 50% | 6 | 25 |

Look at the last row. It held the work that mattered most to the project. It scored lowest.

Imran Qureshi had come over to see why Anaya was frowning. He agreed that by this sum it came last. "So you override it," he said. "Out loud. What is not allowed is pretending the number told you to."

::: key What a score is for
A teacher may give a student more marks for a good reason, and write the reason down. A RICE score works the same way. Its inputs are mostly guesses. The formula knows nothing about strategy, or about which work depends on which. A score helps you organise an argument. It does not replace one. When a team overrides a score, it should write down why. Anaya wrote beside the last row: "It is the wedge. Everything else exists elsewhere."
:::

A second method is better for agreeing scope with other people. Scope is how much work is in or out. *MoSCoW* is like packing a bag for a trip. It sorts every item into Must, Should, Could or Won't take.

Anaya's Musts were the greeting and the finder of fixed-shape numbers. Her Shoulds were names and addresses. Her Coulds were photographs. Her Won'ts for the year were other Indian languages, voice notes, a browser version, and selling to anyone outside Sahaj. Imran read the last column twice. He said it was the first Won't list he had seen that was not embarrassed.

One more idea came from a friend in finance. The *cost of delay* is what you lose for each week an option waits. It is like milk going sour on the shelf. For most items the answer was "a little". For the greeting it was another week of customers being asked for their details. That put it at the front, with no more arithmetic.

## Summary

A vision says where a company is going. A strategy says how it will win. A plan says what happens this quarter. Only the strategy makes you leave things out.

- A strategy has a diagnosis (what the real problem is), a guiding policy (the approach, including what you refuse) and coherent actions that help each other. Taking out the company's name tests whether it is specific.
- A wedge is the small first target that can clearly be won. A North Star is one sentence of customer value and the one number that measures it.
- RICE scores options by reach, impact and confidence, divided by effort. The score organises a decision. You may override it if you write down why.
- MoSCoW agrees scope by sorting items into Must, Should, Could and Won't. The Won't list tells you the most.
- Cost of delay, the loss for each week of waiting, often settles the order where scoring cannot.
