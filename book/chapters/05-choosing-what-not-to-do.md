On Sunday mornings, Anaya telephoned Dr. Meenakshi Rao. Meenakshi had taught her linguistics and was now retired in Bengaluru. In the third week of March, Anaya read out her list of work.

Rewrite the chatbot's greeting. Build a tool to find identity numbers. Extend it to names and addresses. Make it work in Hindi. Make it work in Hinglish. Read photographs. Read voice notes. Sell it to other companies. Build a small version that runs in a browser.

Meenakshi asked which of the nine she would decline to do. Anaya said she would do all of them eventually. "Then you have no strategy," said Meenakshi. "You have a hope with a calendar."

In this chapter we look at what a strategy is made of, and how a team decides where to begin. We also see how to rank competing work without letting a formula make the decision for you.

## The case: nine good ideas

Every item on the list was reasonable, and that was the difficulty. If nothing on a list is ever refused, the list can't guide a decision on Monday morning, because any task can be defended.

Meenakshi's remark wasn't harsh. It was practical. A strategy begins when someone is left out.

## Vision, strategy and plan

Meenakshi asked Anaya to say how three things differ, because people often mix them up. A vision says where you are going. A strategy says how you will win. A plan says what you will do now. The table shows each one for the guard.

Table: Vision, strategy and plan
| | Question it answers | Horizon | The guard's version |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vision | Where are we going? | Three to five years | Every team at every company that talks to customers in India can see at a glance that nothing personal travels further than it should |
| Strategy | How will we win? | One to two years | Start with Sahaj's own chat and nowhere else, and be the best in the country at the mixture of languages people actually type |
| Plan | What will we do now? | This quarter | Ship the number-finding part, in the chat, to the support team, by the end of June |

A vision is allowed to be grand, and a plan is easy to write. The strategy is the hard part. It has to be specific enough to be wrong, and it has to leave things out.

## The parts of a strategy

A strategy has three parts, and each one is there for a reason.

The first is the *diagnosis*. It's an honest account of the real challenge, written so that someone could disagree with it. "We have a privacy problem" is not a diagnosis. Anaya's version said this: personal details reach places they shouldn't, because customers type them freely, the chatbot invites them, nobody has the means to remove them, and existing tools are built for one writing system and fail on the way people here write. It took four drafts before it stopped sounding like a pitch.

The second is the *guiding policy*. This is the overall approach to the challenge, and it includes what you will refuse to do. Hers said: catch details by their shape first, because that is cheap and certain. Handle names and addresses next, because that is where other tools fail. Do all of it inside the company, and in the way people actually write. And don't try to solve everything that could be called privacy.

The third part is a set of coherent actions: a few steps that carry out the policy and support one another. She wrote four, noticed that two didn't depend on the others, and cut them.

::: watch A goal is not a strategy
"Become the leading privacy platform in India" is a goal. It has no diagnosis in it, and it tells nobody what to do on Monday. Here is a quick test. Remove the company's name from the strategy, and ask whether a competitor could paste it into their own document without anyone noticing. A sentence such as "use best-in-class technology to protect customer privacy at scale" passes that test, so it isn't yet a strategy. A version of it had crept into Anaya's second paragraph, and she deleted it.
:::

## Whom it serves, and where to begin

The *value proposition* is a plain statement of whom the product serves, what job it does for them, and why it is better than what they have. Anaya's positioning statement from the last chapter was the first draft. Now it had to carry weight, because the whole strategy rested on it.

Where to begin is a separate decision. Meenakshi's rule was to start narrow enough to be the best at something. A team can widen later. But it can't easily recover from being merely adequate at everything.

The narrow first target that a product can clearly win is called its *wedge*. It should be small enough to be excellent at, and joined to something larger, so that winning it opens a door.

For the guard, the wedge was not "privacy for Indian companies". It was to find and hide the personal details in Sahaj's own support chat, in the three ways people write. That was far smaller than the vision, and entirely winnable. It had a willing customer inside the company and a real body of messages. And it had Farah Sheikh, who would know within a week whether it worked.

### One number

The wedge was paired with a *North Star*. That is one sentence saying what value the customer gets, and one number that measures it.

Here the sentence was that messages leave the chat with nothing personal left in them. The number was the share of messages that do. If the number rose, the work was succeeding. And if Anaya ever caught herself arguing about some other number, she would stop and ask why.

## Naming the product

When Farah heard about the project, she stopped at Anaya's desk. Everyone, she said, was calling it "the privacy thing". If it didn't get a proper name, it would take whichever one was said first in a meeting.

The name had to say what the product was and who it was for. It also had to be easy to say to a customer on the telephone.

They considered a dozen. Farah thinks in the language of her customers, and she proposed Bharat Privacy Guard. She predicted that everyone would shorten it to "the guard" within a week. They chose it, partly because it was exactly as ornate as the name on a signboard.

## Ranking the work

Now that the strategy was in place, Anaya needed an order for the nine items. She used a common scoring method called *RICE*. It rates each option on four questions.

- Reach: how many people or messages does it touch in a given period?
- Impact: how much does it change things for each of them?
- Confidence: as a percentage, how sure are you of the first two answers?
- Effort: how many person-weeks will the work take?

You multiply reach, impact and confidence, and then divide by effort. The table shows four of her options.

Table: RICE scores for four of the options
| Option | Reach | Impact | Confidence | Effort (person-weeks) | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rewrite the greeting | 400 | 1 | 80% | 0.1 | 3,200 |
| Find fixed-shape numbers | 400 | 2 | 80% | 2 | 320 |
| Catch photographs of cards | 30 | 3 | 50% | 1 | 45 |
| Find names and addresses in Hinglish | 150 | 2 | 50% | 6 | 25 |

Look at the last row. It held the work that mattered most to the project, and it scored lowest.

Imran Qureshi had come over to see why Anaya was frowning. He agreed that by this arithmetic it came last. "So you override it," he said. "Out loud. What is not allowed is pretending the number told you to."

::: key What a score is for
The inputs to a RICE score are mostly guesses, and the formula knows nothing about strategy or about which work depends on which. A score organises an argument. It doesn't replace one. When a team overrides a score, it should write down the reason. Anaya wrote beside the last row: "It is the wedge. Everything else exists elsewhere."
:::

A second method is better for agreeing scope with other people. *MoSCoW* sorts every item into Must, Should, Could or Won't.

Anaya's Musts were the greeting and the finder of fixed-shape numbers. Her Shoulds were names and addresses. Her Coulds were photographs. Her Won'ts for the year were other Indian languages, voice notes, a browser version, and selling to anyone outside Sahaj. Imran read the last column twice. He said it was the first Won't list he had seen that wasn't embarrassed.

One more idea came from a friend in finance. The *cost of delay* is what you lose for each week an option waits. For most items the answer was "a little". For the greeting it was another week of customers being asked for their details. That settled its place at the front without any more arithmetic.

## Summary

A vision says where a company is going. A strategy says how it will win. A plan says what happens this quarter. Only the strategy requires you to leave things out.

- A strategy has a diagnosis (what the real challenge is), a guiding policy (the approach, including what you decline) and coherent actions that support one another. Removing the company's name tests whether it is specific.
- A wedge is the narrow first target that can clearly be won. A North Star is one sentence of customer value and the one number that measures it.
- RICE scores options by reach, impact and confidence, divided by effort. The score organises a decision, and you may override it when you write down the reason.
- MoSCoW agrees scope by sorting items into Must, Should, Could and Won't. The Won't list is the most informative.
- Cost of delay, the loss for each week of waiting, often settles the order where scoring doesn't.
