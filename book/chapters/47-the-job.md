Rhea Kapoor telephoned on a Sunday evening in June and began, as people do who have rehearsed an apology, with the end of it. She knew it was Anaya's only free night, she said, and she would not keep her long. Anaya had met her in the autumn at a meetup, where Rhea had asked the one question that nobody else wanted to ask the speaker. Rhea had been an operations analyst in a bank for six years. Since then she had spent a year working through every part of what Anaya had spent a year doing, alone and at night, and she had built something of her own: a small tool that read a week of customer-support tickets, sorted them into themes, and wrote the report that her old manager used to spend a day on. Ten design partners had used it. She had shown the work to nobody who could give her a job.

Rhea had ten job advertisements open, and all of them said "AI product manager". She could not see why she would be right for any of them. Anaya asked her to send the ten and to say what her resume said at the top. After a pause Rhea said that it said she was passionate about AI.

## The case: ten advertisements and one resume

The sections that follow are the work of a few Sunday evenings. They are placed last in the book because they use everything before them, and because the skill they teach, showing what one has done, is the one that most people omit.

## One title, four jobs

The first thing to understand, Anaya told Rhea, is that the title is a label stuck onto at least four different jobs.

Table: Four jobs behind one title
| Kind of company | What it builds | What it wants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adds AI features to an existing product | Features for users it already has | Judgement, a record of shipping, and the ability to work with people who build models |
| Builds something that would not exist without AI | A new product | Speed, prototyping, comfort with a thing that sometimes fails, and a habit of measuring |
| Makes tools, interfaces and models for other teams | Platforms | Technical depth and an understanding of what a developer finds painful |
| Builds AI for its own employees | Internal tools | Someone who can make people change how they work, keep it secure, and show hours saved |

They ask for different evidence, Anaya said, so one resume cannot serve all ten. She asked Rhea to do something dull. Put the ten advertisements across the top of a sheet. Down the left, write every skill any of them mentions. Tick the box where a role asks for a skill. When the sheet is finished, the rows with the most ticks show what the market wants, and the rows with none are noise. This is a *role matrix*. For each row Rhea was to make one further mark, honestly: whether, for her, the skill was proven, partly proven or missing.

It took Rhea three evenings. She sent the sheet back with a note: most of what they want, she had done, and she had not said it in a way that they could check. Anaya told her to say it in terms of what she had proved. "I shipped an AI product to ten real users and raised its useful-result rate from fifty-eight percent to seventy-one" is a sentence, she said. "Passionate about AI" is a mood.

## What goes on the page

They rewrote the resume over two Sundays, and the method was one sentence long. Every line was to say what she did, what she decided and what happened.

Table: Resume lines before and after
| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| Worked on an AI tool for support teams | Built a theme-finding tool for support tickets; chose to ground every theme in quoted phrases, and raised the share of themes marked useful from 58% to 71% across ten partner teams |
| Responsible for testing | Wrote a fixed test set of twenty past weeks and used it to stop two releases that would have lowered accuracy |

The second line, Anaya pointed out, tells the reader that Rhea thinks about quality before she ships. The reader does not need her to say that she does.

A *case study* is a one- or two-page account of one piece of work. Rhea's would answer six questions in order.

1. What was the problem, and what evidence showed it was real?
2. What did you decide, and what did you reject?
3. What did you give up, knowingly?
4. What happened, in numbers?
5. What went wrong, and what did you change?
6. What would you do next?

The fifth question does the work, Anaya said. Anybody can describe a success, and people hire someone who knows what broke. She advised two case studies and not six, placed where a recruiter would find them: a small page of Rhea's own, linked from the top of her profile. The headline of the profile should name the job Rhea wanted and one thing she had proved, with nothing about being a lifelong learner.

## The four questions that every loop asks

In the middle of June they began on the interviews. Anaya started by describing how most interview loops are built. There are four kinds of question, and each has a shape.

Table: Four kinds of interview question
| Kind | An example | A shape to follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product sense | Design a product for busy parents | Clarify the goal, pick a user, list their problems, choose one, propose solutions, rank them, say how you would measure it |
| Strategy | Should we enter the Indian market? | Goal, market, customer, competitors, our advantage, then a recommendation and its risks |
| Metrics | Daily users fell ten percent. Why? | Check the data is right, internal or external cause, which segment, which step, hypotheses, how to test them |
| Execution | Two teams want the same engineer. What do you do? | What each is worth, the options, the trade-offs, a decision, how you tell people |

*Product sense* is the ability to choose a user, find their real problem and propose a sensible solution, and it frightened people most, because it seemed to need an inspiration they did not have. It does not. It needs the habit of choosing a user before proposing anything, and Rhea's habit, after a year of work, was good.

Anaya's advice was to say the structure aloud before starting: something like "I'll clarify, pick a user, find the problems and then choose". The interviewer can follow, and if they want the candidate somewhere else they can say so in the first minute and not the fortieth. And at the end, choose. Interviewers want to watch a candidate commit to a decision and give reasons, and people who list five options and recommend none fail at this more than at anything else.

She rehearsed a metrics question that night. Daily users had fallen ten percent. Rhea began by asking whether the number itself could be wrong: a change to how it was counted, a broken event. That is the first correct move, Anaya said, and most candidates skip it.

## Designing a system out loud

The question that is most particular to the new kind of job, and the one Rhea feared, was the design interview. An interviewer says: design a support assistant for our product. This is the *system-design interview*, and it asks for no code. It asks for a sign that the candidate can think about an AI feature as a whole. Anaya gave her six steps, and Rhea wrote them in the order she would say them.

Table: Six steps for a system-design answer
| Step | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Users and task | Who it is for, the task, and what success looks like |
| 2. Approach | A model alone, or a model with documents to find answers in, or a fixed workflow, or an agent, and why |
| 3. Evaluation | How you would know it worked before launch and after |
| 4. Failure | What happens when it is wrong, slow or attacked |
| 5. Cost and delay | A rough cost for one task, and the delay users will tolerate |
| 6. Rollout | To whom first, what you would measure, and when you would turn it back |

Use numbers, she told Rhea, even rough ones: about two thousand words of context in and three hundred out for each question, so under a rupee. It shows the candidate knows that the thing costs money each time it runs. She had once watched a very good candidate lose a job by never mentioning cost. Rhea said slowly that everything in the year was preparation for this. Anaya said that it was the same year.

## Telling stories about yourself

The last class of question is the oldest: tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer. The interviewer is asking about the candidate's past because it is the best guide they have to the future. Anaya told Rhea to prepare six to eight true stories from real work and to tell each in the same four parts, which people call the *STAR method*: the situation, the task, the action and the result. Most of the time goes to what the candidate did, and not to what the team did, since the interviewer is interviewing the candidate.

They worked on one story for an hour. It was about a decision that had gone wrong, because those are the best.

::: example Rhea's story in four parts
**Situation:** the first version of my tool let an agent choose the steps for the weekly report. **Task:** I owned making it reliable enough for ten design partners. **Action:** I expected the agent to cope better with unusual weeks, so I kept it. When three of my twenty test weeks failed, I built the same flow as a fixed workflow and ran both on the same weeks. **Result:** the workflow failed once in twenty and was twice as fast, so I switched. I learned to measure both options before committing, and I do that now for every large design choice.
:::

It is a good story, Anaya said, because the wrong decision was real and the repair was by measurement and not by apology.

## Alone, and with other people

Two small matters remained. A *take-home* is a task set by an employer to be done alone, in a stated time. The candidate keeps to the time, puts assumptions at the top and ends with what would be done with more time. Reviewers prefer a clear, honest answer to a long one, and a person who says what they left out is trusted more than one who pretends there was nothing.

Networking, which Rhea disliked, worked best when she had something to show. Anaya's advice was not to ask people for a referral. Send a specific person the case study and ask one specific question about it, because that is a question they can answer. Pay was a matter of preparation: learn what the role usually pays in that city, let the company make the first offer, and then ask once, politely, with a reason, such as another offer or the size of the job.

## What happened

It would be pleasant to report that Rhea was hired within the month. She was not. Two of the ten companies asked her back for a second round after the resume and case study had been rewritten as agreed. By the third week of July she was in a room with a woman who asked her to design a support assistant for the company's product. Rhea said aloud, a little shakily, "I'll start with who uses it and what a good outcome is."

Anaya was not there. She heard about it afterwards, in a telephone call in which Rhea described the question and said that halfway through she had realised she was no longer rehearsing. She was explaining something that she had made.

## Summary

"AI product manager" names at least four different jobs, so the useful first step is to put real job advertisements into a role matrix, mark each skill as proven, partly proven or missing, and position oneself on what can be proved.

- Each line of a resume says what was done, what was decided and what resulted. A case study adds the options rejected, the trade-offs, the numbers, what failed and what comes next.
- Interview loops ask four kinds of question: product sense, strategy, metrics and execution. Each is answered by naming a structure aloud and then committing to a choice.
- The system-design interview wants approach, evaluation, failure, cost and rollout, spoken with rough numbers.
- Stories about one's past are told in four parts, with most of the time on one's own action.
- A take-home states its assumptions and what it leaves out, networking starts with a specific question and a piece of work, and a negotiation begins with research and asks once.
