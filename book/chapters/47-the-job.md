Rhea Kapoor phoned on a Sunday evening in June. She began like someone who has practised an apology. She started with the end of it. She knew this was Anaya's only free night, she said. She would not keep her long.

Anaya had met Rhea at a meetup the autumn before. Rhea had asked the one question nobody else dared to ask the speaker. Rhea had spent six years delivering technology programmes for government departments. There was a steering committee (the senior group that approves the plan) every month and a status report every Friday. Then she spent a year doing everything Anaya had done in her year, alone and at night.

She had also built something of her own. It was a small tool that read a week of customer-support tickets and sorted them into themes. Then it wrote the report that a support lead used to spend a day on. Ten design partners had used it. But she had shown it to nobody who could give her a job.

Rhea had ten job advertisements open. All said "AI product manager". She could not see why she would suit any of them. Anaya asked her to send all ten. She also asked what her resume said at the top. After a pause Rhea said it said she was passionate about AI.

## The case: ten advertisements and one resume

The sections below are the work of a few Sunday evenings. They come last in the book because they use everything before. They also teach the skill most people skip: showing what you have done.

## One title, six jobs

Anaya told Rhea the first thing to know. The title is a label stuck on at least six different jobs. It is like the word "shop". A shop can sell shoes, run a repair bench or serve tea. The table shows the six jobs, and what each kind of employer wants from the person it hires.

Table: Six jobs behind one title
| Kind of employer | What it builds | What it wants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adds AI features to an existing product | Features for users it already has | Judgement, a record of shipping, and the ability to work with people who build models |
| Builds something that would not exist without AI | A new product | Speed, prototyping, comfort with a thing that sometimes fails, and a habit of measuring |
| Makes tools, interfaces and models for other teams | Platforms | Technical depth and an understanding of what a developer finds painful |
| Builds AI for its own employees | Internal tools | Someone who can make people change how they work, keep it secure, and show hours saved |
| A consulting firm that builds AI systems for clients | Projects for other organisations, often large ones | Delivery discipline, plain writing, evaluation and governance, and the nerve to tell a client what a system cannot do |
| A public-sector or digital-public-infrastructure body | Services for citizens, often in several languages and sometimes by voice | Field deployments, Indic languages and voice, ministry stakeholders, and open-weight options |

The six jobs ask for different proof, Anaya said. So one resume cannot serve all ten advertisements. For someone who has delivered technology projects, three doors are open. One is a company built on AI. One is a consulting firm that builds AI systems for clients. One is a public-sector body.

Rhea's six years sat closest to the third. Then Anaya asked her to do something dull. Put the ten advertisements across the top of a sheet. Down the left side, write every skill any of them names. Tick the box wherever a role asks for a skill.

When the sheet is full, the rows with the most ticks show what the market wants. The rows with no ticks are noise. This sheet is a *role matrix*. It is like a shopping list ticked against shops. Rhea then added one more mark per row, and it had to be honest. For her, was the skill proven, partly proven or missing?

It took Rhea three evenings. She sent the sheet back with a note. She had done most of what they wanted. She had just not said it in a way anyone could check. Anaya told her to say it as what she had proved. "I shipped an AI product to ten real users and raised its useful-result rate from fifty-eight percent to seventy-one" is a sentence, she said. "Passionate about AI" is a mood.

## What goes on the page

They rewrote the resume over two Sundays. The method fitted in one sentence. Every line says what she did, what she decided and what happened. A resume line is like a shop's price tag. It should say what the thing is and what it costs, not that the shop is "passionate about goods". The table shows two lines before and after.

Table: Resume lines before and after
| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| Worked on an AI tool for support teams | Built a theme-finding tool for support tickets; chose to ground every theme in quoted phrases, and raised the share of themes marked useful from 58% to 71% across ten partner teams |
| Responsible for testing | Wrote a fixed test set of twenty past weeks and used it to stop two releases that would have lowered accuracy |

The second line, Anaya pointed out, tells the reader that Rhea thinks about quality before she ships. The reader does not need her to say so.

Next came the *case study*. It is a one- or two-page story of one piece of work, with a lesson. Rhea's would answer six questions, in order.

1. What was the problem, and what evidence showed it was real?
2. What did you decide, and what did you reject?
3. What did you give up, knowingly?
4. What happened, in numbers?
5. What went wrong, and what did you change?
6. What would you do next?

The fifth question does the work, Anaya said. Anyone can describe a win. People hire someone who knows what broke. She advised two case studies, not six.

They should sit where a recruiter will find them: a small page of Rhea's own, linked from the top of her profile. The profile's headline should name the job Rhea wanted and one thing she had proved. It should say nothing about being a lifelong learner.

## The four questions that every loop asks

In the middle of June they began on interviews. Anaya first explained how most interview loops (the set of rounds a company puts you through) are built. There are four kinds of question. Each has a shape you can follow, like a recipe.

Table: Four kinds of interview question
| Kind | An example | A shape to follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product sense | Design a voice assistant for a state helpline, in two languages | Clarify the goal, pick a user, list their problems, choose one, propose solutions, rank them, say how you would measure it |
| Strategy | Should we enter the Indian market? | Goal, market, customer, competitors, our advantage, then a recommendation and its risks |
| Metrics | Daily users fell ten percent. Why? | Check the data is right, internal or external cause, which segment, which step, hypotheses, how to test them |
| Execution | Two teams want the same engineer. What do you do? | What each is worth, the options, the trade-offs, a decision, how you tell people |

*Product sense* is choosing a user first, then finding their real problem and proposing a sensible fix. It is like asking who the guest is before you decide what to cook. It frightened people most, because it seemed to need a spark they did not have. It does not. It needs one habit: choose a user before you propose anything. After a year of work, Rhea's habit was good.

She practised on a prompt from a world she knew: a voice assistant for a state helpline, in two languages. Her first three moves were short. She clarified the goal. It was fewer callers waiting and fewer wrong answers about a scheme's rules, not a clever assistant.

She chose one user: a caller on a basic phone who speaks only the regional language and rings at the end of a working day. She listed that caller's problems before naming any feature. The caller does not know which scheme applies. The caller does not know which papers to bring. The caller gets cut off when the line is bad. Only then did she say which of the three she would solve first.

Anaya advised her to say the structure aloud before starting. For example: "I'll clarify, pick a user, find the problems and then choose." Then the interviewer can follow. If they want the candidate somewhere else, they can say so in the first minute, not the fortieth.

And at the end, choose. Interviewers want to watch a candidate commit to a decision and give reasons. People who list five options and recommend none fail here more than anywhere.

That night she rehearsed a metrics question. Daily users had fallen ten percent. Rhea began by asking whether the number itself could be wrong. Maybe the way it was counted had changed. Maybe an event had broken. That is the first correct move, Anaya said, and most candidates skip it.

## Designing a system out loud

The question most special to the new kind of job was the design interview. Rhea feared it. The interviewer says: design a support assistant for our product. This is the *system-design interview*. It asks for no code. It is like planning a house aloud, not laying the bricks. It asks for a sign that the candidate can think about an AI feature as a whole.

Anaya gave her six steps. Rhea wrote them in the order she would say them.

Table: Six steps for a system-design answer
| Step | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Users and task | Who it is for, the task, and what success looks like |
| 2. Approach | A model alone, or a model with documents to find answers in, or a fixed workflow, or an agent, and why |
| 3. Evaluation | How you would know it worked before launch and after |
| 4. Failure | What happens when it is wrong, slow or attacked |
| 5. Cost and delay | A rough cost for one task, and the delay users will tolerate |
| 6. Rollout | To whom first, what you would measure, and when you would turn it back |

Use numbers, she told Rhea, even rough ones. For example: about two thousand words of context in and three hundred out for each question, so under a rupee. Numbers show the candidate knows the thing costs money every time it runs. Anaya had once watched a very good candidate lose a job by never mentioning cost.

Rhea said slowly that the whole year had been preparation for this. Anaya said it was the same year.

## Telling stories about yourself

The last kind of question is the oldest: tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer. The interviewer asks about the past because it is the best guide to the future.

Anaya told Rhea to prepare six to eight true stories from real work. She was to tell each one in the same four parts. This is the *STAR method*. It is a four-box form: the situation, the task, the action and the result. Most of the time goes to what the candidate did, not what the team did. The interviewer is interviewing the candidate.

They worked on one story for an hour. It was about a decision that went wrong, because those make the best stories.

::: example Rhea's story in four parts
**Situation:** the first version of my tool let an agent choose the steps for the weekly report. **Task:** I owned making it reliable enough for ten design partners. **Action:** I expected the agent to cope better with unusual weeks, so I kept it. When three of my twenty test weeks failed, I built the same flow as a fixed workflow and ran both on the same weeks. **Result:** the workflow failed once in twenty and was twice as fast, so I switched. I learned to measure both options before committing, and I do that now for every large design choice.
:::

It is a good story, Anaya said. The wrong decision was real, and the fix came from measuring, not from saying sorry.

## The question about her title

In the second round the interviewer put down the script and picked up the resume. Six years of programme delivery for government departments, he said, sounds like programme management. Why should he believe she could own a product?

Anaya had expected it. They had rehearsed it on a Sunday. The answer was not to argue with the label. It was to name three decisions Rhea had owned. The first was what she refused to build. Her tool could have answered questions about the report in a chat box. She left that out because she could not measure it.

The second was the threshold that stopped a release. Her fixed test set had held back two versions. The third was the number she answered for: the share of themes marked useful, which went from 58 to 71 percent.

Then Rhea turned her history into an asset. Six years in a regulated sector meant audits, data-location rules and sign-offs from people who could say no. Most candidates would have to claim that from a reading list. She did not say she had been a product manager. She said she had owned an outcome, and she could show the number.

## Building while they watch

Some loops add a round where the candidate builds something small, live, with a coding assistant, while the interviewer watches. It tests judgement more than typing. It is like a cooking round where they taste as you go.

Anaya's advice came from the verifier idea earlier in the book. Before you ask the assistant for anything, say how you will check its work. Say the check aloud, such as "the result must hide every number in my ten sample messages". Ask for small steps.

Read what comes back, and say what you would not trust yet. Interviewers watch whether the candidate can tell a change that worked from one that only looks right. A half-finished build whose checks pass beats a finished one that nobody checked.

## Alone, and with other people

Two small matters remained. The first is the *take-home*. It is homework from an employer, done alone in a stated time. Keep to the time. Put your assumptions at the top. End with what you would do with more time. Reviewers prefer a clear, honest answer to a long one. A person who says what they left out is trusted more than one who pretends nothing was left out.

The second is networking, which Rhea disliked. It worked best when she had something to show. Anaya's advice was not to ask people for a referral. Instead, send a specific person the case study and ask one specific question about it. That is a question they can answer.

Pay is a matter of preparation. Learn what the role usually pays in that city. Let the company make the first offer. Then ask once, politely, with a reason, such as another offer or the size of the job.

## What happened

It would be nice to say Rhea was hired within the month. She was not. Two of the ten companies asked her back for a second round, after the resume and case study had been rewritten as agreed.

By the third week of July she was in a room with a woman who asked her to design a support assistant for the company's product. Rhea said aloud, a little shakily, "I'll start with who uses it and what a good outcome is."

Anaya was not there. She heard about it afterwards, on a phone call. Rhea described the question. She said that halfway through she realised she was no longer rehearsing. She was explaining something she had made.

::: key What you can now say
Fill each N from your own project.
- My case study names what broke, N things, and the number that moved, from N to N.
- I priced it by who pays: one message costs N rupees to serve, and the margin stays above N percent at ten times the base use.
- I count what people do without keeping what they wrote: N measures, and none of them stores a message.
:::

## Summary

"AI product manager" names at least six different jobs. So the first useful step is to put real job advertisements into a role matrix. Mark each skill as proven, partly proven or missing. Then position yourself on what you can prove.

- Each resume line says what was done, what was decided and what resulted. A case study adds the options rejected, the trade-offs, the numbers, what failed and what comes next.
- Interview loops ask four kinds of question: product sense, strategy, metrics and execution. Answer each by naming a structure aloud, then committing to a choice.
- The system-design interview wants approach, evaluation, failure, cost and rollout, spoken with rough numbers.
- If your resume reads like programme management, answer with three decisions you owned and a number. Do not argue about the label. In a live build round, state the check before you ask the assistant for the work.
- Tell stories about your past in four parts, with most of the time on your own action.
- A take-home states its assumptions and what it leaves out. Networking starts with a specific question and a piece of work. A negotiation begins with research, and asks once.
