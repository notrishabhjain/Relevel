Rhea Kapoor telephoned on a Sunday evening in June. She began, as people do who have rehearsed an apology, with the end of it. She knew this was Anaya's only free night, she said, and she wouldn't keep her long.

Anaya had met Rhea in the autumn at a meetup, where Rhea had asked the one question nobody else wanted to ask the speaker. Rhea had been an operations analyst in a bank for six years. Since then she had spent a year working through every part of what Anaya had spent a year doing, alone and at night. And she had built something of her own: a small tool that read a week of customer-support tickets, sorted them into themes, and wrote the report her old manager used to spend a day on. Ten design partners had used it. But she had shown the work to nobody who could give her a job.

Rhea had ten job advertisements open, and all of them said "AI product manager". She couldn't see why she would be right for any of them. Anaya asked her to send the ten, and to say what her resume said at the top. After a pause Rhea said that it said she was passionate about AI.

## The case: ten advertisements and one resume

The sections that follow are the work of a few Sunday evenings. They come last in the book because they use everything before them. They also teach the skill that most people leave out: showing what you have done.

## One title, four jobs

The first thing to understand, Anaya told Rhea, is that the title is a label stuck onto at least four different jobs. The table shows the four, and what each kind of company wants from the person it hires.

Table: Four jobs behind one title
| Kind of company | What it builds | What it wants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adds AI features to an existing product | Features for users it already has | Judgement, a record of shipping, and the ability to work with people who build models |
| Builds something that would not exist without AI | A new product | Speed, prototyping, comfort with a thing that sometimes fails, and a habit of measuring |
| Makes tools, interfaces and models for other teams | Platforms | Technical depth and an understanding of what a developer finds painful |
| Builds AI for its own employees | Internal tools | Someone who can make people change how they work, keep it secure, and show hours saved |

The four ask for different evidence, Anaya said, so one resume can't serve all ten advertisements. Then she asked Rhea to do something dull. Put the ten advertisements across the top of a sheet. Down the left side, write every skill any of them mentions. Tick the box wherever a role asks for a skill.

When the sheet is finished, the rows with the most ticks show what the market wants. The rows with none are noise. This sheet is a *role matrix*. For each row, Rhea was to make one more mark, and to make it honestly: for her, was the skill proven, partly proven or missing?

It took Rhea three evenings. She sent the sheet back with a note: she had done most of what they wanted, and she hadn't said it in a way anyone could check. Anaya told her to say it in terms of what she had proved. "I shipped an AI product to ten real users and raised its useful-result rate from fifty-eight percent to seventy-one" is a sentence, she said. "Passionate about AI" is a mood.

## What goes on the page

They rewrote the resume over two Sundays, and the method was one sentence long. Every line should say what she did, what she decided and what happened. The table shows two lines before and after.

Table: Resume lines before and after
| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| Worked on an AI tool for support teams | Built a theme-finding tool for support tickets; chose to ground every theme in quoted phrases, and raised the share of themes marked useful from 58% to 71% across ten partner teams |
| Responsible for testing | Wrote a fixed test set of twenty past weeks and used it to stop two releases that would have lowered accuracy |

The second line, Anaya pointed out, tells the reader that Rhea thinks about quality before she ships. The reader doesn't need her to say it.

Next came the *case study*: a one- or two-page account of one piece of work. Rhea's would answer six questions, in order.

1. What was the problem, and what evidence showed it was real?
2. What did you decide, and what did you reject?
3. What did you give up, knowingly?
4. What happened, in numbers?
5. What went wrong, and what did you change?
6. What would you do next?

The fifth question does the work, Anaya said. Anybody can describe a success, and people hire someone who knows what broke. She advised two case studies, not six. They should sit where a recruiter will find them: a small page of Rhea's own, linked from the top of her profile. The headline of the profile should name the job Rhea wanted and one thing she had proved. It should say nothing about being a lifelong learner.

## The four questions that every loop asks

In the middle of June they began on the interviews. Anaya started by describing how most interview loops are built. There are four kinds of question, and each has a shape you can follow.

Table: Four kinds of interview question
| Kind | An example | A shape to follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product sense | Design a product for busy parents | Clarify the goal, pick a user, list their problems, choose one, propose solutions, rank them, say how you would measure it |
| Strategy | Should we enter the Indian market? | Goal, market, customer, competitors, our advantage, then a recommendation and its risks |
| Metrics | Daily users fell ten percent. Why? | Check the data is right, internal or external cause, which segment, which step, hypotheses, how to test them |
| Execution | Two teams want the same engineer. What do you do? | What each is worth, the options, the trade-offs, a decision, how you tell people |

*Product sense* is the ability to choose a user, find their real problem and propose a sensible solution. It frightened people most, because it seemed to need an inspiration they didn't have. It doesn't. It needs the habit of choosing a user before proposing anything, and after a year of work, Rhea's habit was good.

Anaya's advice was to say the structure aloud before starting: something like "I'll clarify, pick a user, find the problems and then choose". That way the interviewer can follow. If they want the candidate somewhere else, they can say so in the first minute and not the fortieth.

And at the end, choose. Interviewers want to watch a candidate commit to a decision and give reasons. People who list five options and recommend none fail at this more than at anything else.

She rehearsed a metrics question that night. Daily users had fallen ten percent. Rhea began by asking whether the number itself could be wrong: a change to how it was counted, or a broken event. That is the first correct move, Anaya said, and most candidates skip it.

## Designing a system out loud

The question most particular to the new kind of job, and the one Rhea feared, was the design interview. An interviewer says: design a support assistant for our product. This is the *system-design interview*, and it asks for no code. It asks for a sign that the candidate can think about an AI feature as a whole.

Anaya gave her six steps, and Rhea wrote them in the order she would say them.

Table: Six steps for a system-design answer
| Step | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Users and task | Who it is for, the task, and what success looks like |
| 2. Approach | A model alone, or a model with documents to find answers in, or a fixed workflow, or an agent, and why |
| 3. Evaluation | How you would know it worked before launch and after |
| 4. Failure | What happens when it is wrong, slow or attacked |
| 5. Cost and delay | A rough cost for one task, and the delay users will tolerate |
| 6. Rollout | To whom first, what you would measure, and when you would turn it back |

Use numbers, she told Rhea, even rough ones. For example: about two thousand words of context in and three hundred out for each question, so under a rupee. Numbers show that the candidate knows the thing costs money each time it runs. Anaya had once watched a very good candidate lose a job by never mentioning cost.

Rhea said slowly that everything in the year had been preparation for this. Anaya said it was the same year.

## Telling stories about yourself

The last kind of question is the oldest: tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer. The interviewer asks about the candidate's past because it is the best guide to the future they have.

Anaya told Rhea to prepare six to eight true stories from real work, and to tell each one in the same four parts. People call this the *STAR method*: the situation, the task, the action and the result. Most of the time goes to what the candidate did, not to what the team did, because the interviewer is interviewing the candidate.

They worked on one story for an hour. It was about a decision that had gone wrong, because those make the best stories.

::: example Rhea's story in four parts
**Situation:** the first version of my tool let an agent choose the steps for the weekly report. **Task:** I owned making it reliable enough for ten design partners. **Action:** I expected the agent to cope better with unusual weeks, so I kept it. When three of my twenty test weeks failed, I built the same flow as a fixed workflow and ran both on the same weeks. **Result:** the workflow failed once in twenty and was twice as fast, so I switched. I learned to measure both options before committing, and I do that now for every large design choice.
:::

It is a good story, Anaya said, because the wrong decision was real, and the repair came from measuring, not from apologising.

## Alone, and with other people

Two small matters remained. The first is the *take-home*: a task set by an employer to be done alone, in a stated time. Keep to the time. Put your assumptions at the top, and end with what you would do with more time. Reviewers prefer a clear, honest answer to a long one. A person who says what they left out is trusted more than one who pretends there was nothing.

The second is networking, which Rhea disliked. It worked best when she had something to show. Anaya's advice was not to ask people for a referral. Instead, send a specific person the case study and ask one specific question about it, because that is a question they can answer.

Pay was a matter of preparation. Learn what the role usually pays in that city. Let the company make the first offer. Then ask once, politely, with a reason, such as another offer or the size of the job.

## What happened

It would be pleasant to report that Rhea was hired within the month. She wasn't. Two of the ten companies asked her back for a second round, after the resume and case study had been rewritten as agreed. By the third week of July she was in a room with a woman who asked her to design a support assistant for the company's product. Rhea said aloud, a little shakily, "I'll start with who uses it and what a good outcome is."

Anaya wasn't there. She heard about it afterwards, in a telephone call in which Rhea described the question and said that halfway through she had realised she was no longer rehearsing. She was explaining something she had made.

## Summary

"AI product manager" names at least four different jobs. So the useful first step is to put real job advertisements into a role matrix, mark each skill as proven, partly proven or missing, and position yourself on what you can prove.

- Each line of a resume says what was done, what was decided and what resulted. A case study adds the options rejected, the trade-offs, the numbers, what failed and what comes next.
- Interview loops ask four kinds of question: product sense, strategy, metrics and execution. Each is answered by naming a structure aloud and then committing to a choice.
- The system-design interview wants approach, evaluation, failure, cost and rollout, spoken with rough numbers.
- Stories about your past are told in four parts, with most of the time on your own action.
- A take-home states its assumptions and what it leaves out. Networking starts with a specific question and a piece of work. A negotiation begins with research, and asks once.
