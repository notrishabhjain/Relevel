Rhea Kapoor telephoned on a Sunday evening in June, and began, as people do who have rehearsed an apology, with the end of it. "I know it's your only free night. I won't keep you."

Anaya had met her in the autumn, at a meetup where Rhea had asked the one question that nobody else wanted to ask the speaker. She had been an operations analyst in a bank for six years. Since then she had spent a year working through every part of what Anaya had spent a year doing, on her own, at night, and she had built something of her own. It was a small tool that read a week of customer-support tickets, sorted them into themes, and wrote the report that her old manager used to spend a day on. Ten design partners had used it. She had shown the work to nobody who could give her a job.

"I have ten job advertisements open," said Rhea. "They all say 'AI product manager.' And I can't see why I would be right for any of them."

"Send me the ten," said Anaya. "And tell me what your resume says at the top."

There was a pause on the line. "It says I'm passionate about AI."

## One title, four jobs

The first thing to understand, Anaya said, was that the title was a label stuck on at least four different jobs.

One kind of company adds AI features to a product that already exists, for users it already has. It wants judgement, a record of shipping, and the ability to work with people who build models. Another kind builds something that would not exist without AI, and wants speed, prototyping, comfort with a thing that sometimes fails, and a habit of measuring. A third makes tools, interfaces and models for other teams to build on, and wants technical depth and an understanding of what a developer finds painful. The fourth builds AI for its own employees, and wants someone who can make people change how they work, keep it secure, and show hours saved.

"They ask for different evidence," said Anaya. "So you can't write one resume for all ten."

She asked Rhea to do something dull. Put the ten advertisements across the top of a sheet. Down the left, write every skill any of them mentions. Tick the box where a role asks for a skill. When she had finished, the rows with the most ticks showed what the market wanted, and the rows with none were noise. This was a **role matrix**. And for each row Rhea was to make one further mark, honestly: whether, for her, the skill was *proven*, *partly proven* or *missing*.

It took Rhea three evenings. She sent the sheet back with a note. "Most of what they want, I've done. I just haven't said it in a way they could check."

"Then say it," said Anaya, "in terms of what you proved. 'I shipped an AI product to ten real users and raised its useful-result rate from fifty-eight percent to seventy-one' is a sentence. 'Passionate about AI' is a mood."

## What goes on the page

They rewrote the resume over two Sundays, and the method was one sentence long. Every line was to say what she did, what she decided, and what happened.

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| Worked on an AI tool for support teams | Built a theme-finding tool for support tickets; chose to ground every theme in quoted phrases, and raised the share of themes marked useful from 58% to 71% across ten partner teams |
| Responsible for testing | Wrote a fixed test set of twenty past weeks and used it to stop two releases that would have lowered accuracy |

"Look at the second one," said Anaya. "It tells the reader you think about quality before you ship. They don't need you to say you do."

Then the case study. A **case study** is a one- or two-page account of one piece of work. Rhea's would answer, in order, six questions. What was the problem, and what evidence showed it was real? What did you decide, and what did you reject? What did you give up, knowingly? What happened, in numbers? What went wrong, and what did you change? What would you do next?

"The fifth is the one that does the work," Anaya said. "Anybody can describe a success. People hire someone who knows what broke."

She advised two case studies and not six, put where a recruiter would find them: a small page of her own, linked from the top of her profile. The headline of the profile, she said, should name the job Rhea wanted and one thing she had proven. Nothing about being a lifelong learner.

## The four questions that every loop asks

In the middle of June they began on the interviews, and Anaya started by telling her how most of the loops were built. There would be four kinds of question, and each had a shape.

| Kind | An example | A shape to follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product sense | Design a product for busy parents | Clarify the goal, pick a user, list their problems, choose one, propose solutions, rank them, say how you would measure it |
| Strategy | Should we enter the Indian market? | Goal, market, customer, competitors, our advantage, then a recommendation and its risks |
| Metrics | Daily users fell ten percent. Why? | Check the data is right, internal or external cause, which segment, which step, hypotheses, how to test them |
| Execution | Two teams want the same engineer. What do you do? | What each is worth, the options, the trade-offs, a decision, how you tell people |

The first, **product sense**, was the one that frightened people most, because it seemed to need an inspiration they did not have. It does not. It needs the habit of choosing a user before proposing anything, and Rhea's habit, after a year of work, was good.

"Say your structure out loud before you start," said Anaya. "Something like 'I'll clarify, pick a user, find the problems and then choose.' The interviewer can follow you, and if they want you somewhere else they can say so in the first minute and not the fortieth." She stopped, and added the part she thought mattered more. "And at the end, choose. They want to watch you commit to a decision and give your reasons. People who list five options and recommend none fail at this more than at anything."

She ran the metrics question on her, one night, as a rehearsal. *Daily users fell ten percent.* Rhea began by asking whether the number itself could be wrong: a change to how it was counted, a broken event. That was the first correct move, and most candidates skip it.

## Designing a system out loud

The question that was most special to the new kind of job, and the one Rhea feared, was the design interview. An interviewer would say: *design a support assistant for our product.* It is called a **system-design interview**, and what is wanted is not code. It is a sign that you can think about an AI feature as a whole.

Anaya gave her six steps, and Rhea wrote them down in the order she would say them.

Users and the task, and what success looks like. The approach: ask the model alone, or give it documents to find answers in, or build a fixed workflow, or an agent, and why. How you would know it worked before and after launch. What happens when it is wrong, slow or attacked. A rough cost for one task, and the delay users will tolerate. And how you would roll it out: to whom first, what you would measure, and when you would turn it back.

"Use numbers," she said. "Rough ones. Say, about two thousand words of context in and three hundred out for each question, so under a rupee. It shows you know the thing costs money each time it runs. I once watched a very good candidate lose a job by never mentioning cost."

"Everything in the year is preparation for this," said Rhea slowly.

"Everything. It is the same year."

## Telling stories about yourself

The last class of question was the oldest. *Tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer.* The interviewer is asking about your past because it is the best guide they have to your future.

Anaya told her to prepare six to eight true stories from real work, and to tell each in the same four parts, which people call the **STAR method**: the situation, the task, the action, and the result. "Spend most of the time on what you did," she said. "Not what the team did. They're interviewing you."

They worked on one story for an hour. It was about a decision that had gone wrong, because those are the best.

*Situation: the first version of my tool let an agent choose the steps for the weekly report. Task: I owned making it reliable enough for ten design partners. Action: I expected the agent to cope better with unusual weeks, so I kept it. When three of my twenty test weeks failed, I built the same flow as a fixed workflow and ran both on the same weeks. Result: the workflow failed once in twenty and was twice as fast. I switched. I learned to measure both options before committing, and I do that now for every large design choice.*

"It's good," said Anaya, "because the wrong decision was real, and the repair was by measurement and not by apology."

## Alone, and with other people

Two small things remained, and she gave them quickly.

A **take-home** is a task set by an employer to be done alone, in a stated time. Keep to the time. Put your assumptions at the top. End with what you would do with more time. Reviewers prefer a clear, honest answer to a long one, and a person who says what they left out is trusted more than one who pretends there was nothing.

And networking, which Rhea disliked, worked best when she had something to show. "Don't ask people for a referral," said Anaya. "Send a specific person your case study and ask one specific question about it. You'll get replies, because it's a question they can answer."

Pay, she said, was a matter of preparation. Learn what the role usually pays in that city. Let the company make the first offer. Then ask once, politely, with a reason: another offer, or the size of the job.

## What happened

It would be pleasant to report that Rhea was hired within the month. She was not. Two of the ten companies asked her back for a second round, once the resume and the case study had been rewritten as they had agreed. By the third week of July she was in a room with a woman who asked her to design a support assistant for the company's product.

Rhea said, aloud and a little shakily, "I'll start with who uses it and what a good outcome is."

Anaya, who was not there, heard about it afterwards, in a telephone call, in which Rhea described the question and said that halfway through she had realised she was no longer rehearsing. She was explaining something she had made.

## What to carry forward

"AI product manager" names at least four different jobs, so the useful first step is to put real job advertisements into a role matrix, mark each skill as proven, partly proven or missing, and position yourself on what you can prove. Each line of a resume says what you did, what you decided and what resulted; a case study adds the options rejected, the trade-offs, the numbers, what failed and what comes next. Loops ask four kinds of question, product sense, strategy, metrics and execution, each answered by naming a structure aloud and then committing to a choice. The system-design interview wants approach, evaluation, failure, cost and rollout, spoken with rough numbers. Stories about your past are told in four parts, with most of the time on your own action. A take-home states its assumptions and what it leaves out, networking starts with a specific question and a piece of work, and a negotiation begins with research and asks once.
