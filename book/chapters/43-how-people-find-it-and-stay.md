In the third week of January, five developers sat in a row on one side of a table. Each had a laptop. Each wore the faintly guilty look of someone in an experiment, who is not sure what is being measured.

Anaya had given them one sheet of paper. It was the README, the first page of instructions for the developer kit. They had nothing else. There was no demo, no call and no help. A stopwatch lay on the table.

She had said, "Add this to a small chat program and clean one message. Tell me when you are done. I won't help you." Then she went to stand by the window with her hands behind her back. It was the only way to keep them still.

The kit was live. Mr. Menon's company had signed a trial. What mattered now was whether anyone else could use it. This chapter tells what the stopwatch showed. It also tells what that taught her about how people find a product and stay with it.

## The case: five developers and a stopwatch

Think of a shop with a leaky door. Pulling more people in does not help if they all walk out again. Imran Qureshi had warned her what she would learn. He had a sentence he liked: if they don't stay, more sign-ups only means losing people faster.

## A funnel and its limits

Think of a kitchen funnel. A lot goes in at the top, and only a little comes out at the bottom. Growth has five stages, with a short name: *AARRR*. The letters stand for acquisition, activation, retention, referral and revenue. The first job at each stage is to name one event you can count. The table gives the guard's event for each.

Table: The five stages and the guard's event for each
| Stage | The question | The guard's event |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acquisition | Did they arrive? | A developer opens the demo page and pastes a message |
| Activation | Did they get value? | The kit cleans a message inside their own project |
| Retention | Did they come back? | It is still running, and cleaning messages, four weeks later |
| Referral | Did they bring others? | A colleague at another company opens the demo page after a shared link |
| Revenue | Did they pay? | They move from the trial to a plan |

Anaya knew the picture was a simplification. It looks like a straight line. Real people arrive through shared links and return after months. It also tempts a team to fix things at the top of the funnel. Yet the stage that usually decides whether a product works is retention. The funnel puts it fourth.

## The first moment of value

Think of the first sip of good tea. Until then, you have only waited. That morning, Anaya cared most about the measure that decides whether anyone reaches the later stages. It is the *activation moment*. This is the first time a user gets the value the product promises.

For the guard, it was a developer seeing their own message come out the other side. It had an Aadhaar number in it, and the number was gone. Everything before that moment is cost to the user. It is effort spent before anything good has happened. How long it takes to reach the moment is the *time-to-value*. You count it from the moment the person starts.

The stopwatch gave her the times. She wrote them down as they came.

Table: How far the five developers got
| Step | Developers who finished | Median time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open the README | 5 of 5 | 1 minute |
| Create an account and get a key | 4 of 5 | 7 minutes |
| Install the kit | 4 of 5 | 11 minutes |
| Clean the first message | 3 of 5 | 24 minutes |

The median is the middle time when you line them up in order. One developer stopped at the account. Another got lost at the fourth step. Three reached the moment of value, after twenty-four minutes at the median. The developer who left at the first obstacle had written a short note. Anaya read it after they had gone: "I'd have left here too." Imran looked over her shoulder. He said people don't leave at the hard part. They leave before it.

The remedy came to her over the next few days. What if the first moment of value came before the account? The demo page could let a developer paste a message of their own and see it cleaned. There would be nothing to sign up for. Then the activation moment would come ahead of the hard step. By the time they were asked for an email, they would have seen it work.

## A teardown over dinner

Think of taking apart a clock to see how it ticks. That week Anaya did something she had put off for three months. She took apart a product that had already solved this problem. She chose one that Farah used all the time. It was a spelling and writing assistant that sat inside whatever she was typing.

They went through it stage by stage, on Farah's sofa, with her laptop between them. At each stage they noted what happened, and what could be copied.

Table: A teardown of a writing assistant
| Stage | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| Acquisition | People saw its suggestions in the tools they already used, and a free plan spread it |
| Activation | The first underlined suggestion in her own writing, within seconds |
| Retention | It lived where people already write, so there was no new habit to form |
| Revenue | The more advanced suggestions sat behind a paid plan |

Farah asked what Anaya would copy. "The moment of value," Anaya said slowly. It happens in the user's own work. It does not happen on a separate page, or after a setup.

She saw that the kit's value lived inside the developer's own chat program. The demo page stood in for that, and it was a good stand-in. But real activation would come when the kit was in their code. The aim was to shorten the road between the first and the second.

## What makes someone act

Think of a school bell. A child goes to class when she wants to, can walk there, and hears the bell. For retention (people coming back), Anaya turned to a model Dr. Meenakshi Rao had described from the study of behaviour. A person does something when three things meet at the same instant. They want to. They are able to. Something prompts them. This is the *Fogg behaviour model*.

Its rule is useful. When a behaviour is not happening, check the three in reverse order. A missing prompt is the cheapest to fix. Next comes ability. Last comes motivation, which is the hardest to move.

For a compliance buyer (the person who answers for the rules), the prompt was easy to see. It was a Monday email with one line: "This week the guard hid 1,204 details across 87,000 messages. Two were flagged for review." It took no effort to open. It reminded the buyer why they had the tool. For a developer, the prompt was the best README they had ever read. The ability was how little was left to do.

The team also watched the retention curve for each week's new users. A line that falls and then flattens means someone has found a lasting use. A line that falls to nothing means they have not.

## How a customer arrives

Think of three ways a customer reaches a shop. She walks in on her own. A salesman visits her home. Or she tries a thing herself, and a salesman comes later to close the deal. Anaya drew the three ways a customer can go from hearing of a product to paying for it.

Table: Three routes to a paying customer
| Route | How it works | When it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product-led growth | People sign up and get value on their own, and some of them upgrade | The value shows in minutes and the price is low |
| Sales-led | A person runs demonstrations and a contract | The deal is large or a security review is required |
| In between | Individuals adopt the product first, and a salesperson arrives when their team grows | A product that one person can try but a team must approve |

The guard sat in the middle. A single developer could try the demo alone. But connecting a whole chat system meant going through the security lead whom Mr. Menon called "the Wall". That, she saw, was exactly the moment when a person would be useful.

## A badge, and a line she would not cross

Think of the sticker on a shop door that says "We accept UPI". Other shoppers see it and learn about the service. The last matter was a loop. A *growth loop* is a cycle where one user's actions bring in the next user. The output feeds back in as input. Anaya thought of a small mark at the bottom of a chat window: "Protected by Bharat Privacy Guard", with a link. A customer's customer would see it. Another developer might follow it.

She was careful with two terms. When users bring in other users, that is *virality*. When a product gets better for everyone as more people use it, that is a *network effect*. Many products have the first and not the second. She suspected hers was one of them.

Positioning decided which loop could work at all. Positioning means the place your product holds in people's minds. A badge made sense because the guard was for companies that talk to customers in India. A company that wanted to look careful would be glad of the mark.

Then she wrote a rule in the margin, in the same hand as "rarer is not fixed".

::: watch Dark patterns are out
A *dark pattern* is like a shop with the exit hidden. It is a design that tricks a user into acting against their own interest. Examples are a box ticked in advance, a made-up shortage, or a cancel button nobody can find. Such designs can lift a number for a quarter.

They also destroy the trust the whole product rests on. Anaya told Imran that the customer could switch the badge off without asking. He said it would cost some visibility. "Yes," she said. "We are a privacy tool. We shouldn't be the ones who hide the exit."
:::

## Summary

Think of the shop again. People come in, try something, come back, bring friends and pay. Growth has five stages worth counting: acquisition, activation, retention, referral and revenue. The one that usually decides whether a product works is the one that comes fourth.

- The activation moment is the first time a user gets the value promised. It should come as early as possible, because everything before it is cost to the user. Time-to-value measures the road. The best place for the moment is inside the user's own work.
- A behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet. When it is not happening, check them in reverse order.
- Customers may arrive by themselves, through a salesperson or through both. The right route depends on how quickly the value shows and how large the deal is.
- A growth loop can bring in users, but virality is not a network effect. A design that tricks people, a dark pattern, may raise a number. It destroys the trust that retention depends on.

Growth only helps if you can count it honestly. That is the subject of the next chapter.
