There are two ways to learn how an AI product is made. The first way is to build one. You write a few lines of code. You send a question to a machine. You see what breaks. Then you measure how badly. The course app is made for this way. This book does not take its place.

The second way is to learn the whole subject in order. Someone explains it one step at a time. New words stop feeling strange. Slowly they turn into a picture in your head. Think of it as learning a city. The app lets you walk its lanes. The book gives you the map. This book is that second way.

This book is for someone who has delivered technology projects. Now you want to own an AI product. You do not need to write code. You do not need to know statistics. Here is the rhythm. Every week, for twenty-six weeks, you read one chapter. That takes about ten minutes. Then you do the app chapter named under its title.

Anaya has a team around her. You have the app. When Imran runs something, the app gives you the button. When Lakshmi asks how she would know, the app asks you the same question. These are the check questions. By the end, you can say what you built. You can say how you know it works. You can say it with your own numbers.

The book covers the same ground as the app. It uses the same order and the same terms (the special words of the subject). It has no exercises and no code to type. Instead it tells one story from start to finish. An idea sticks better when it is tied to a real decision. It is like remembering a road by the day you got lost on it.

## The case

Sahaj is a company in Pune. Its app lets people pay their electricity bills. Lately it also lets them apply for small loans. Inside the app there is a chatbot. A chatbot is a helper that types back answers. This one answers customers' questions.

One day in March, a product manager named Anaya Deshmukh sees something odd. Customers keep typing their Aadhaar numbers into the chatbot. Nobody asked them to. It is like people reading out their bank PIN to a shop clerk who only asked the time.

The book has forty-seven chapters. In them, Anaya and her colleagues build a small tool. It is called Bharat Privacy Guard. The tool spots personal details. It works in English and in Hindi. It also works in the mix of the two that most people type. Then it stops those details from travelling further than they should.

The tool is only a way to tell the story. The real subject is what Anaya must do along the way. She must decide what is worth building. She must learn what an AI system is made of. She must check whether it works.

She must work out what it costs. She must learn how it can fail. She must set limits on what it may do. In the end she must put it in front of real users. Each chapter takes up one of these problems. It comes at the moment the project needs it.

## How the book is organised

Think of the book as a train with forty-seven coaches. The coaches are numbered in one line. They are split into seven parts. The table below shows what each part is about.

| 1 | 1 to 7 | Choosing a problem and planning the work |
| 2 | 8 to 13 | What a modern AI system is, and how to talk to one |
| 3 | 14 to 19 | Giving the machine documents, and putting a whole system together |
| 4 | 20 to 26 | Tools, reasoning, testing at a large scale, and cost |
| 5 | 27 to 32 | Messy data, privacy, rules, and planning for failure |
| 6 | 33 to 39 | How the machinery works, and how to keep it safe |
| 7 | 40 to 47 | Putting a product out, earning money from it, and working in the field |

Every chapter has the same layout, so you always know where you are. It is like a train whose coaches all look alike. A short list at the top says what the chapter covers. The first paragraphs tell a piece of the story. Then come numbered sections. They explain the ideas behind the story. Definitions, examples and tables sit apart in boxes. A summary ends the chapter. A list of key terms comes after it.

## How to read it

Read the chapters in order. Think of a staircase. You cannot reach step five without step four. Each chapter starts where the last one stopped. Each one uses only words that earlier chapters have already explained. The book is checked for this. If a word shows up before the chapter that explains it, that counts as a mistake.

A new term is highlighted the first time it appears. That is in the chapter that explains it. Rest your pointer on it and its meaning pops up. The key terms at the end of each chapter repeat the meanings. The glossary at the back lists every term in A to Z order. A glossary is a small dictionary of the book's own words. Next to each term is the chapter where it first appears.

Under each chapter title, you will see which app chapters cover the same ground. Go there when you want to practise.

You do not need to know anything about AI, programming or business to start.

## A note on the case

Sahaj, Anaya, her colleagues and the customers are all made up. So are the numbers they work with. The tools and methods are real. So are the kinds of failure in the story. It is like a film "based on real life". The faces are actors. The troubles are true.

Nothing in this book is legal advice. The tool in the story helps a company collect less private information. It also helps the company pass on less. The book never says the tool makes anyone follow a law. No tool of that kind can. Think of a lock on a door. It helps, but it does not make you honest.
