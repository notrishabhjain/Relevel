There are two ways to learn how an AI product is made. One is to make one: write a few lines of code, send a question to a machine, break something, measure what broke. That is what the course app is for, and nothing in this book replaces it. The other way is to sit down with the whole subject, slowly, in a chair, and read until the pile of unfamiliar words turns into a picture you can hold in your head. This book is the second way.

It covers the same ground as the course app, in the same order, with the same words. It has no exercises, no steps to follow and no code to type. What it has instead is a story, because ideas stay with a person longer when they arrive attached to someone who needed them.

## The story

A company called Sahaj runs a small app in Pune where people pay electricity bills and, lately, take loans. A chatbot in the corner of the app answers their questions. One evening a product manager named Anaya Deshmukh notices that customers keep typing their Aadhaar numbers into it, though nobody asked them to.

That is the whole beginning. Over the forty-odd chapters that follow, Anaya and the people around her try to build a small tool that notices personal details in English, in Hindi and in the mixture of the two that most of India actually types, and keeps them from travelling further than they should. The tool is called Bharat Privacy Guard. To build it, Anaya has to learn what a product manager does when the problem is vague, what an AI system is made of, how to tell whether it works, what it costs, how it fails, what it is safe to let it do, and how to put it in front of real people and keep it there.

Every one of those lessons is in the course app. Here you meet each one at the moment somebody needs it.

## How to read it

Read the chapters in order. Each one picks up what the last one left unsettled, and each one uses words the earlier chapters have already explained. Nothing is used before it is taught; the book is checked for that.

The first time a new word appears it is softly highlighted, and the plain meaning waits underneath when you hover over it. At the end of each chapter, a short box gathers the words that chapter taught. The back of the book has them all, in alphabetical order, with the chapter where each first appeared.

Under every chapter title there is a line saying which chapters of the course app it retells. If a chapter makes you want to try something with your own hands, that line is the door.

You do not need to know anything about AI, about programming or about business to begin. You need to be willing to read a sentence twice.

## A note on honesty

Sahaj, Anaya, and everyone you meet at the company are invented. So are their customers and their numbers. The ideas are real, the tools named are real, and the things that go wrong in these pages go wrong in real systems all the time.

Nothing here is legal advice. The tool at the centre of the story helps an organisation collect and pass on less of people's private information than it otherwise would. It is never described in these pages as making anyone compliant with a law, because no tool of that kind can.

## The people

**Anaya Deshmukh** is a product manager at Sahaj. She is good at listening and at writing things down, less good at admitting what she does not know, and she is about to be taught that by a machine.

**Imran Qureshi** is the lead engineer. He built most of the plumbing behind the chatbot. He speaks quietly, draws on napkins, and distrusts any sentence that contains the word "just".

**Lakshmi Iyer** runs compliance. She worked in a bank for twenty years before joining Sahaj, and she has a way of asking "and how would we know?" that has ended many good ideas and saved a few better ones.

**Farah Sheikh** leads the support team. She reads more of what customers actually write than anyone else in the building, and she can tell you which of them are lying about their age.

**Dr. Meenakshi Rao** taught Anaya linguistics, a long time ago, and is now retired and still asking questions. Anaya calls her on Sunday mornings.
